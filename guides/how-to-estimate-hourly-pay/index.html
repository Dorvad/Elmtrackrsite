How to estimate your hourly pay

To estimate hourly pay, multiply your paid hours by your base rate, then add extra for any overtime hours and any premium hours at their higher rates. The figure you get is an estimate — a useful check on your payslip, but not the same as official payroll, which also applies taxes and deductions.

Last reviewed: 2026-07-16

The pieces that make up the estimate

  • Base hourly rate — what one ordinary hour pays.
  • Paid minutes — the time you are actually paid for, which is where breaks matter.
  • Unpaid breaks — time on site that is not paid, and so is left out of paid minutes.
  • Overtime tiers — a higher rate once you pass a daily or weekly hours threshold.
  • Premiums — a higher rate for when you work, such as weekends, nights or holidays.

Step by step

1. Start from paid hours

Take the total time worked and subtract any unpaid breaks. What remains is your paid time.

2. Apply the base rate

Multiply regular paid hours by your base hourly rate.

3. Add overtime

For hours past your overtime threshold, apply the overtime multiplier instead of the base rate.

4. Add premiums

For hours that fall in a premium window — a weekend or night, say — apply that premium. A single hour can be both overtime and a premium.

5. Read it as an estimate

The total is an estimated gross, before tax and deductions. Treat it as a figure to compare, not a final number.

Estimate vs official payroll

An estimate is built from hours and rates. Official payroll starts from the same idea but then applies income tax, social contributions, benefits and any agreements specific to your employer — so the number on your payslip is normally lower than an estimated gross, and can differ for legitimate reasons.

Common sources of discrepancy

  • Unpaid breaks counted differently than you expected.
  • An overtime threshold or multiplier that does not match your contract.
  • A premium window (weekend or night hours) defined differently by your employer.
  • Rounding of start and end times.
  • Taxes and deductions, which an estimate of gross pay does not include.

Frequently asked questions

Is estimated pay the same as take-home pay?

No. An estimate is a gross figure from hours and rates. Take-home pay is what remains after tax and deductions, which an estimate does not include.

How do unpaid breaks affect the estimate?

They reduce paid time. Subtract unpaid breaks from total time before applying your rate, or your estimate will be too high.

Can one hour be both overtime and a premium?

Yes. Overtime depends on how many hours you have worked; a premium depends on when you work. A weekend hour past your threshold can be both.

Why is my payslip different from the estimate?

Common reasons include taxes and deductions, a different break or overtime rule, or rounding. A difference is not automatically an error — it is a prompt to check.
